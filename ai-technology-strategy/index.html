Useful AI. On your terms.

A good AI strategy is not a shopping list of tools. It is a set of choices about what is useful, what should stay human and what your organisation is willing to depend on.

For charities, social enterprises and public-interest teams trying to make sense of changing technology without losing sight of their purpose.

The real challenge

You don't need to adopt everything

There is enormous pressure to do something with AI. But the questions that matter are more familiar: which problems are worth solving? What happens to people's information? Who checks the work? Can staff disagree with a recommendation? And what happens if a provider changes its terms?

For a small team, technology choices have consequences beyond an IT budget. They shape workload, trust, accessibility, data protection and the confidence people have in their own judgement. Strategy is about making those trade-offs visible before a tool becomes part of everyday practice.

This might be useful if…

  • Your team is already experimenting with AI, but guidance has not caught up.
  • Trustees or leaders want to understand opportunities and risks without jargon.
  • You are reassessing subscriptions, data sharing or dependence on a provider.
  • You want learning and confidence, not another technology project nobody owns.
What we can work on

Decisions, safeguards and useful practice

Priorities before products

Start with the work your organisation actually needs to do. Identify where AI may help, where it adds complexity and where doing less is better.

Responsible AI policy

Develop practical principles for confidentiality, transparency, human review, accessibility, procurement and accountability — written so people can use them.

Technology sovereignty

Examine ownership, portability, contracts, costs and open alternatives. Reduce avoidable lock-in without pretending every organisation can self-host everything.

Staff and trustee learning

Facilitate conversations and hands-on exploration for people with different starting points. Build judgement rather than a dependence on prompts.

Small, bounded experiments

Define a question, test a use case on suitable information, collect feedback and decide whether to stop, adapt or proceed.

Review and governance

Establish ownership, decision records and review points so technology remains a choice the organisation can revisit.

How we work

A sensible path from uncertainty to decisions

Listen and map

Understand the purpose, everyday workflows, existing tools and concerns. Start with what people actually do, not vendor demonstrations.

Choose and try

Agree a few priorities and clear boundaries. Test something proportionate, or write the policy and decision framework first if that is what is missing.

Learn and retain control

Capture what changed, who is accountable and when to review the choice. Leave people with a practical approach they can maintain.

Common questions

A few things worth asking

Do we need an AI policy before anyone starts experimenting?

You need clear boundaries immediately, particularly around personal or confidential information. A useful policy can grow with what you learn; it does not need to be a large document before the first sensible conversation.

How do we decide what is safe to try?

Begin with the consequence of getting something wrong. Consider data sensitivity, affected people, reversibility, accessibility and who can genuinely check the output. High-stakes decisions deserve much stronger scrutiny than an internal draft.

What if the best decision is not to use AI?

That is a perfectly good outcome. Sometimes improving a process, understanding the data or buying a simpler tool is the more useful choice.
